A boy at the bottom of a well. Thrown there by his own brothers. And yet, years later, he will hold the keys to the food stores of an entire nation — and the same brothers will come to him asking for help, without knowing who he is.

This is the story of Prophet Yusuf, peace be upon him. And the Quran itself calls it "the best of stories."

Surah Yusuf is the twelfth chapter of the Quran, with 111 verses. What makes it unusual is this: for most prophets, parts of their story appear across many different surahs. Yusuf's life is told in this one surah, in full, and in order. So today we'll follow it the way it's told, verse by verse.

It begins with a dream. Yusuf tells his father, Prophet Yaqub: "O my father, indeed I have seen eleven stars and the sun and the moon; I saw them prostrating to me."

Scholars of tafsir explain the eleven stars as his eleven brothers, and the sun and the moon as his father and mother. Yaqub understands immediately that this dream means something big. And his first reaction is protective. He tells his son: don't tell this to your brothers, or they will plot against you.

He was right to worry. The brothers already felt their father loved Yusuf and his younger brother more than them. Their resentment turns into a plan. One says, kill him or send him to some far land. Another says: don't kill him — throw him into the bottom of a well, and some travellers will pick him up.

They go to their father and ask to take Yusuf out with them, so he can eat well and play, and they promise to guard him. And Yaqub says something heartbreaking: it saddens me that you take him, and I fear a wolf will eat him while you aren't paying attention.

And they do it. They put him into the well. But in that moment the Quran tells us Allah inspired Yusuf: one day you will tell them about what they did, while they don't realise who you are. Remember that line. The whole story is moving toward it.

That night the brothers return to their father, weeping. They say a wolf ate him. And they bring his shirt, stained with false blood.

Yaqub's answer is one of the most quoted lines in the surah: "So patience is most fitting. And Allah is the one sought for help against that which you describe."

A caravan stops at the well. Their water-drawer lowers his bucket — and shouts, "Good news! Here is a boy." They hide him as merchandise and sell him cheaply, for a few dirhams.

He ends up in Egypt, in the house of an important man, who tells his wife to treat him well — perhaps he'll benefit us. And when Yusuf grows up, the Quran says Allah gave him judgment and knowledge.

Then comes a hard test. The wife of his master tries to seduce him. He refuses and says, "I seek the refuge of Allah." When the truth is examined, his shirt is found torn from the back — proof he was pulling away, not advancing. He's innocent. But he still ends up in prison, and he tells Allah he would rather have prison than what he is being called to.

Think about that choice. Comfort with sin, or prison with his integrity. He chose prison.

In prison, two young men tell him their dreams, and he interprets them. But before he does, he speaks to them about faith — he says he follows the religion of his fathers, Ibrahim, Ishaq and Yaqub. Even in a cell, he's teaching.

Later, the king of Egypt has a disturbing dream: seven fat cows being eaten by seven lean ones. Nobody can explain it. Then one of those former prisoners remembers Yusuf.

Yusuf's interpretation is practical: seven years of good harvest, then seven years of hardship. Store the grain in the ear so it lasts. It's not just a prophecy — it's a food-security plan.

And the king asks for him. Yusuf asks to be put in charge of the storehouses of the land, saying he is a knowing guardian. The boy from the well now manages Egypt's food.

When the hard years come, famine reaches Yaqub's family too. The brothers travel to Egypt to buy food — and stand in front of Yusuf. He recognises them. They don't recognise him.

The Quran tells of how he brings his younger brother to stay with him, and of their father's grief, which grows so deep his eyes turn white with sorrow. And still Yaqub says: I only complain of my suffering and my grief to Allah. He never gives up hope.

Finally, the moment. The brothers stand before him again, humbled. He asks if they remember what they did to Yusuf. And they realise. "Are you indeed Yusuf?" And he says: "I am Yusuf, and this is my brother."

They admit their wrong. And his reply is the line this whole story builds to: "No blame will there be upon you today. Allah will forgive you."

No revenge. No speech about everything he suffered. Forgiveness, immediately.

He sends his shirt to his father, and when it's cast over Yaqub's face, his sight returns. The family comes to Egypt. Yusuf raises his parents up to the throne, and they all bow down — and he says, this is the meaning of my dream from before. The eleven stars, the sun and the moon.

The surah ends by saying there is a lesson in these stories for people of understanding. Patience from Yaqub. Integrity from Yusuf in the house of his master. Trust in Allah in the well and in the prison. And at the end, forgiveness — when he had every power to take revenge.
