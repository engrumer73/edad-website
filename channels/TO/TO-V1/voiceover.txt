In the middle of December, there's a night when you can step outside, look up, and — if your sky is dark enough — see a shooting star every minute or so. Sometimes more.

It's the Geminid meteor shower. And 2026 is a particularly good year to catch it. Let me tell you exactly when, where, and how.

The Geminids peak on the night of the 13th into the 14th of December, 2026. But you don't need to hit one exact minute. The shower has a broad peak — rates of more than 100 meteors per hour can last 10 to 12 hours. And it's active for days either side: roughly the 4th to the 17th of December.

You'll see slightly different peak times quoted. EarthSky gives a predicted peak around 5:44 UTC on the 14th. The International Meteor Organization's forecast, reported by Star Walk and Spain's National Geographic Institute, centres it later that day, around 14:00 UTC. My advice: don't stress about the exact hour. Go out on the night of the 13th, and if you can, the night of the 14th too.

The biggest enemy of a meteor shower is the Moon. A bright Moon washes out the fainter meteors. This year, the Moon is only a thin waxing crescent on peak night. In the UK, the Royal Observatory says it sets around 9:20 in the evening — after that, the sky is properly dark. And the next first-quarter Moon doesn't arrive until the 17th.

So in 2026, the Moon is basically giving you the night off.

The meteors seem to fly out from one point in the sky called the radiant, in the constellation Gemini, near its two bright stars, Castor and Pollux.

But here's a tip a lot of people miss: you don't need to stare at Gemini. Meteors can appear anywhere in the sky; they just trace back to that point. Look at a wide, dark patch of sky, about halfway up.

The Geminids also have a nice bonus: they're the one major shower that shows good activity before midnight. Many sources say the best time is around 2 a.m. local time, when the radiant is high. But you'll see meteors from mid-evening onward.

They favour the Northern Hemisphere, but they're also visible from southern latitudes.

Now, honesty time. You'll often read "120 meteors per hour." That's the zenithal hourly rate — what you'd see under a perfectly dark sky with the radiant straight overhead. The American Meteor Society lists a theoretical maximum of around 150.

In real life, you'll see fewer. Under a dark rural sky, maybe 75 to 150 an hour; from the suburbs, more like 25 to 50. That's still a lot of shooting stars. City centre? You'll catch a few of the brightest ones. Get out of town if you possibly can.

You don't need a telescope or binoculars — they'd actually make it worse, because they shrink your view. Just your eyes.

Find somewhere dark, away from streetlights. Dress far warmer than you think — December nights are cold, and you'll be sitting still. A reclining chair or a blanket on the ground lets you look up without hurting your neck. Put your phone away, or turn on its red-light or night mode, because every glance at a bright screen resets your eyes' adjustment to the dark. Give yourself at least 20 minutes for your eyes to adapt, and then be patient. Meteors come in clusters, then quiet spells.

One more thing that makes the Geminids special. Most meteor showers come from comets — icy objects leaving trails of dust. The Geminids' parent is an asteroid called 3200 Phaethon.

The meteors are tiny bits of debris from Phaethon hitting our atmosphere at around 35 kilometres per second. That's the light you see — a grain of space dust burning up high above you.
