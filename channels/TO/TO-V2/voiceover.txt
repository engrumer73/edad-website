If you live in the Northern Hemisphere, your shortest day of the year is coming. But here's a puzzle: your earliest sunset has probably already happened, a couple of weeks before it.

That sounds wrong. Let's fix that — and explain what the solstice actually is.

First, the date. The December solstice in 2026 happens on Monday, the 21st of December, at 20:50 UTC. That's 3:50 in the afternoon in New York, 8:50 in the evening in London.

And here's something people often don't realise: the solstice isn't a whole day. It's a single moment, shared by the entire planet at the same instant. So in parts of Asia and Australia, the calendar has already turned to the 22nd.

At that moment, the Sun reaches its southernmost point in our sky for the year. It's directly overhead at about 23.5 degrees south — the Tropic of Capricorn.

After that moment, the Sun starts heading north again. That's why so many cultures saw this as a turning point: the light is coming back.

Why does it happen? Not because we're farther from the Sun. In fact, Earth is closest to the Sun in early January — in 2026, perihelion fell on the 3rd of January. So winter in the north happens while we're nearly at our closest.

The real reason is tilt. Earth's axis is tipped over by about 23.4 degrees. In December, the Northern Hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun. Sunlight arrives at a low angle, spreads out over more ground, and the days are short. Six months later, the north tilts toward the Sun, and it flips.

So the same moment means opposite things depending on where you live. In the north, it's the winter solstice and the shortest day. In the south, it's the summer solstice and the longest day. In Sydney or Buenos Aires, people get around fourteen and a half hours of daylight on that date.

Now, that puzzle from the start. If the 21st is the shortest day, why isn't it also the earliest sunset?

Because our clocks and the Sun don't keep perfectly the same time. Over the year, the moment the Sun is highest in the sky — solar noon — drifts earlier and later against the clock. EarthSky explains that this mismatch between clock time and Sun time is why the Northern Hemisphere's earliest sunset comes before the December solstice.

The order is always the same: earliest sunset in early December, shortest day at the solstice, latest sunrise around early January. The exact dates depend on your latitude.

So if your evenings already feel like they're getting a tiny bit longer before Christmas — you're not imagining it.

You might also see the solstice called "December 21st" as if it's fixed. It's not. Because our calendar year and Earth's orbit don't line up perfectly, the December solstice falls between the 20th and the 23rd. The next one on the 20th of December won't happen until 2080 — and the next on the 23rd not until 2303.

People have marked this turning point for thousands of years. In Scandinavia, there was the pre-Christian Feast of Juul — Yule — at the time of the December solstice. And under the old Julian calendar, the winter solstice fell on the 25th of December.

So on the 21st of December, take a second at sunset. The Sun has gone as far south as it will go this year — and from here, the days only get longer.
