It's lunchtime on a Wednesday in January, in Boston. The North End is busy: the harbour, the warehouses, an elevated railway running past.

And then, without warning, a giant steel tank, fifty feet tall, splits open — and more than two million gallons of molasses come out all at once.

This really happened. It's called the Great Molasses Flood. And as strange as it sounds, it changed the way America builds things.

The date was the 15th of January, 1919, at about 12:30 in the afternoon. The place: the North End of Boston, at 529 Commercial Street.

The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, which had been bought in 1917 by a bigger firm, the United States Industrial Alcohol Company. Why so much molasses? Because molasses can be fermented into ethanol — used in alcoholic drinks, and also a key ingredient in munitions. During the First World War, that was big business.

The tank was built in 1915, about 50 feet tall and 90 feet across, able to hold 2.3 million US gallons — about 8.7 million litres. It was put right by the harbour, where ships brought molasses in from Puerto Rico and further south, and close to the railway tracks.

Three days before the disaster, on the 12th of January, a ship pumped 1.3 million gallons into it. On the day it failed, it held roughly 2.3 million gallons, weighing around 26 million pounds.

And there were warning signs. People living nearby said the tank leaked — molasses oozed down the seams. Locals even collected it. The company's response, according to accounts of the disaster, was to paint the tank brown, which made the leaks harder to see.

You don't need to be an engineer to see the problem with that solution.

When the tank failed, the wave was enormous. Estimates put it somewhere between 15 and 40 feet high, moving at around 35 miles an hour. History Today describes it as a wave about 15 feet high and 160 feet wide.

It knocked buildings off their foundations. It bent the steel supports of the elevated railway. It wrecked a fire station.

Twenty-one people died. About 150 were injured.

Now here's the part that makes this disaster so unusual. Water flows away. Molasses doesn't — not easily. It's thick and heavy. In fact, per gallon, molasses weighs almost one and a half times as much as seawater.

And it was January. As molasses cools, it gets thicker and stickier. So once the wave had spread out and slowed down in the cold air, people caught in it found it almost impossible to move. Rescuers struggled to reach them. The same thing that made it sweet made it deadly.

So whose fault was it? The company's lawyers had an answer ready: it wasn't us. They claimed the tank had been bombed by Italian anarchists. This was 1919; fear of radical bombings was real, and it was a convenient story.

But the court didn't buy it.

Families of the victims and people who had been injured filed 119 claims, and those were combined into what the Massachusetts Secretary of State calls the largest class action lawsuit in the state's history.

The case is remembered for something else too. It was one of the first to use expert witnesses — engineers and scientists explaining in court why the tank failed.

And this is why a sticky, almost comic-sounding disaster matters. NPR describes the case as the catalyst for new regulations governing architects and engineers. Britannica notes that states across the country went on to adopt stricter construction codes.

In other words: the next time someone signs off on a big tank, a bridge or a building, someone qualified has to check the calculations and stand behind them. That idea feels obvious now. In 1919, Boston learned it the hard way.

If you walk through the North End today, there's not much to see. There's a small plaque where the tank once stood. And there's a baseball field — which, by coincidence, roughly echoes the round shape of the tank that stood there.

Twenty-one lives lost, in one of the strangest disasters in American history — and a shift in how engineering is regulated that still protects people today.
