Imagine waking up in June, walking out to your fields, and finding snow on your crops. Not frost. Snow. In 1816, across New England, that really happened — about half a foot of it, in a single day, in June.

People at the time had no idea why. The cause was on the other side of the planet, and it had happened a year earlier.

This is the story of the year without a summer.

In 1815, Mount Tambora stood on the island of Sumbawa, in what's now Indonesia — then part of the Dutch East Indies. It was a tall stratovolcano, around 14,000 feet, and it had been quiet for centuries.

Then, in April 1815, it woke up. The eruption reached its violent peak on the 10th of April. It went on for about two weeks.

To put it on a scale: volcanologists use the Volcanic Explosivity Index. Mount St. Helens and Vesuvius both score a 5. Krakatoa scores a 6. Tambora is a 7. It's the most powerful eruption in recorded human history.

Around the mountain, the human cost was enormous. Tens of thousands of people died from the eruption and the famine that followed; EarthDate, a programme from the University of Texas, puts the figure near 100,000. We'll keep that with the respect it deserves, and follow how the effect travelled.

Here's the part most people miss. It wasn't mainly the ash that changed the weather. Tambora pushed huge amounts of sulfur dioxide high into the atmosphere. Up there it turns into tiny droplets of sulfuric acid, and those droplets reflect sunlight back into space — for years.

Less sunlight in, cooler planet. So the following year, 1816, the Northern Hemisphere got what scientists now call a volcanic winter — right through spring and summer.

In May 1816, frost killed many of the crops already planted in the northeastern United States. Then came that June snowfall. Farmers replanted, and lost crops again. Food got scarce and prices rose on markets from Ireland to China.

And the strange thing? The world didn't connect the dots. Most people didn't link the bad weather to a volcano in Indonesia until decades later. To them it was just a cursed year.

Meanwhile, something odd was happening up north. While much of the world cooled, the Arctic warmed so much that a lot of the sea ice around Greenland melted. Climate doesn't move as one block. It shuffles.

Now for my favourite bit, because a miserable summer gave us some remarkable things.

In June 1816, a group of young writers were staying on Lake Geneva in Switzerland. The weather was so gloomy they were stuck indoors, and they held a contest — who could write the best ghost story.

One of them was a nineteen-year-old named Mary Shelley. The story she started that summer became Frankenstein. Another guest, John Polidori, wrote The Vampyre, one of the first vampire stories in English.

Art changed too. Sunsets after a big eruption can be spectacular, because the particles scatter the light. Art historians believe those glowing skies influenced the British painter J. M. W. Turner and his famous hazy, luminous colours.

There's even a theory about bicycles. In 1817, a German inventor named Karl Drais showed off his "running machine" — a two-wheeler you pushed along with your feet. Some historians link it to the horse-feed shortages after the failed harvests, though that connection is debated. So file that under "maybe."

The good news first. The atmosphere cleaned itself up within about a decade, and life went back to normal.

And Tambora? It's still active. Its last eruption was in 1967, a small one. Scientists keep watching volcanoes like it, because an eruption that size would hit food supplies worldwide. It's possible. It's just not something experts expect in our lifetimes.

So the next time someone complains that summer was "a bit cold this year" — tell them about 1816. Snow in June. Crops gone twice. And a teenager in a rainy Swiss house, writing a monster story that's still in print two hundred years later.
