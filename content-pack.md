### GT-V1 — The Man Whose Name Became "Algorithm" (Mon 30 Nov)

**Title:** The Man Whose Name Became "Algorithm" | Al-Khwarizmi

**Thumbnail:** 9th-century manuscript glowing beside a phone screen. Text: "He named your apps."

**Description:** Every app runs on "the algorithm" — but that word is a man. Meet Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi, the scholar of Baghdad's House of Wisdom who also gave us the word algebra. Sources below.

**Tags:** al-Khwarizmi, algorithm origin, algebra history, House of Wisdom, Islamic Golden Age, Muslim scientists, Baghdad history, history of mathematics

**Chapters:** Intro · Who he was · Baghdad and al-Ma'mun · The book of al-jabr · Completing the square · The lost book and "algorithm" · Beyond mathematics · Outro

**Music:** Audio Library → Ambient or Cinematic, Mood: Calm; low oud or strings, ducked 18–20 dB under voice.

**Voiceover**

&#91;Cold open: phone screen scrolling a feed, slow dissolve to a 9th-century manuscript.\]

Every time your phone decides what to show you next, someone calls it "the algorithm." Funny thing is, that word is a person. A real man, who lived in Baghdad more than eleven hundred years ago. And most people who say his name every day have never heard of him.

His name was Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi. Let's talk about who he was — and how his name ended up in your pocket.

&#91;Title card. Map: Khwarazm, south of the Aral Sea, arrow to Baghdad.\]

We don't know much about his early life. He was born around 780 and died around 850, and his name tells us where his family came from: Khwarazm, a region in Central Asia. \[1\] Some scholars think he may actually have come from a small town near Baghdad. \[2\] Either way, Baghdad is where his story happens.

And here's something you should know up front. There is no portrait of al-Khwarizmi. None survives. When the Soviet Union put him on a postage stamp in 1983, for his 1,200th birthday, the face was imagined. \[3\] So if you ever see "his picture" online — that's an artist's guess.

&#91;Visual: the 1983 stamp, labelled "artist's impression."\]

Now, Baghdad in the early 800s. The Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun was a scholar himself. \[4\] Under him, a place called Bayt al-Hikmah — the House of Wisdom — became a centre for collecting books from across the empire and beyond, translating them into Arabic, and publishing new research. \[1\]\[5\] Greek mathematics, Indian numerals, Persian astronomy, all landing on the same desks.

One of al-Khwarizmi's colleagues, al-Hajjaj, had already translated Euclid's *Elements* into Arabic. \[6\] That's the kind of room he was working in.

&#91;Music lifts slightly.\]

Around the year 830, al-Ma'mun encouraged al-Khwarizmi to write a book on calculation. \[3\] The book he wrote has a long title — *al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wal-muqabala*. "The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing." \[1\]

Hear that word in the middle? *Al-jabr*. Completion. Restoring something to make it whole. When the book was translated into Latin in the 12th century, that word came along with it — and it became "algebra." \[1\]

So before we even get to algorithms: the word algebra is also his.

&#91;Visual: the Arabic word الجبر morphing into "algebra."\]

What was actually in the book? Practical problems. Inheritance. Trade. Measuring land. \[6\] And a method every school student still learns: solving quadratic equations by "completing the square." He didn't just give rules — he gave geometric proofs, drawn as squares and rectangles, to show why the rules work. \[2\]

His most famous example goes like this, and I love that he wrote it in words, not symbols: "a square and ten roots equal thirty-nine." Today we'd write x squared plus ten x equals thirty-nine.

&#91;Animation: square of side x, two 5-by-x strips attached, missing 5-by-5 corner filled in.\]

Picture a square with side x. Add ten x as two strips, five wide each, on two sides. You've got an L shape worth thirty-nine. The corner that's missing is five by five — twenty-five. Fill it in, and you've got a perfect big square worth sixty-four. Its side is eight. Take away the five, and x is three.

That's it. That's "completion." Twelve centuries old, and still on exam papers.

&#91;Pause. Music drops out for one beat.\]

One more thing about that book. It opens with a dedication full of praise for God and the Prophet, peace be upon him. Historians point to that pious preface as evidence of his faith. \[2\]\[7\] For him, careful counting was not separate from belief.

&#91;Section card: "The second book."\]

Now, the word *algorithm*. That comes from a different book — one on arithmetic with the Indian numerals, the 1, 2, 3 system we use today. Here's the twist: the Arabic original is lost. It survives only in a Latin translation, which begins with the words *Algoritmi de numero Indorum* — roughly, "Al-Khwarizmi on the Hindu art of reckoning." \[1\]

*Algoritmi*. That's his name, in Latin. Over time, European scholars used "algorism" for calculating with these numerals, and eventually "algorithm" came to mean any step-by-step procedure. \[1\]\[2\]

&#91;Visual: Latin manuscript; then modern code on a screen.\]

And it's not just English. In Spanish, *guarismo* means a digit. In Portuguese, *algarismo*. Both come from his name too. \[7\]

&#91;Section card: "Beyond mathematics."\]

It would be easy to think of him as just a mathematician. He wasn't. His Arabic-language works on astronomy and geography were hugely influential too. \[2\] He was a scholar of the whole sky and the whole map, not only the page of numbers — which is exactly what the House of Wisdom was built to produce.

So think about that. A man in 9th-century Baghdad, working in a house of books, writing about inheritance shares and land — and today his name is attached to the code that recommends your videos and the maths your kids do for homework.

&#91;Outro. Slow push-in on a manuscript page.\]

The House of Wisdom gave the world a lot more than one mathematician. We'll go inside it soon — who worked there, what they translated, and what happened to it. If you want to follow the golden thread of Islamic history with us, subscribe, and tell me in the comments: which scholar should we cover next?

### GT-V2 — Prophet Yusuf: From the Well to the Treasury (Thu 3 Dec)

**Title:** The Story of Prophet Yusuf (AS) — From the Well to the Treasury of Egypt

**Thumbnail:** an empty desert well at dusk, a single shaft of light; calligraphy يوسف. Text: "The best of stories."

**Description:** The only prophet whose whole life story is told in one surah, in order. Surah Yusuf, retold verse by verse with references. Translation: Sahih International.

**Tags:** Prophet Yusuf, Surah Yusuf, Quran stories, story of Joseph Islam, Islamic history, prophets in Islam, Quran explained, Yaqub

**Chapters:** Intro · The dream · The brothers' plan · The well · Sold in Egypt · The test and the prison · The king's dream · The brothers return · "No blame upon you today" · The dream fulfilled

**Music:** Audio Library → Ambient, Mood: Calm / Sad for the well and Jacob's grief; no percussion. Many viewers prefer no music on Quran-story videos — use soft ambient pads only, or nature sound (wind, water) instead.

**Voiceover**

&#91;Cold open: wind over desert, a rope dropping into a dark well.\]

A boy at the bottom of a well. Thrown there by his own brothers. And yet, years later, he will hold the keys to the food stores of an entire nation — and the same brothers will come to him asking for help, without knowing who he is.

This is the story of Prophet Yusuf, peace be upon him. And the Quran itself calls it "the best of stories." \[1\]

&#91;Title card.\]

Surah Yusuf is the twelfth chapter of the Quran, with 111 verses. \[2\] What makes it unusual is this: for most prophets, parts of their story appear across many different surahs. Yusuf's life is told in this one surah, in full, and in order. \[2\] So today we'll follow it the way it's told, verse by verse.

&#91;On screen: "Surah Yusuf 12:4".\]

It begins with a dream. Yusuf tells his father, Prophet Yaqub: "O my father, indeed I have seen eleven stars and the sun and the moon; I saw them prostrating to me." \[3\]

Scholars of tafsir explain the eleven stars as his eleven brothers, and the sun and the moon as his father and mother. \[4\] Yaqub understands immediately that this dream means something big. And his first reaction is protective. He tells his son: don't tell this to your brothers, or they will plot against you. \[3\]

&#91;Section card: "The brothers' plan."\]

He was right to worry. The brothers already felt their father loved Yusuf and his younger brother more than them. \[3\] Their resentment turns into a plan. One says, kill him or send him to some far land. \[3\] Another says: don't kill him — throw him into the bottom of a well, and some travellers will pick him up. \[3\]

They go to their father and ask to take Yusuf out with them, so he can eat well and play, and they promise to guard him. \[3\] And Yaqub says something heartbreaking: it saddens me that you take him, and I fear a wolf will eat him while you aren't paying attention. \[3\]

&#91;Visual: a shepherd's path, long shadows.\]

And they do it. They put him into the well. But in that moment the Quran tells us Allah inspired Yusuf: one day you will tell them about what they did, while they don't realise who you are. \[3\] Remember that line. The whole story is moving toward it.

That night the brothers return to their father, weeping. They say a wolf ate him. \[3\] And they bring his shirt, stained with false blood. \[3\]

Yaqub's answer is one of the most quoted lines in the surah: "So patience is most fitting. And Allah is the one sought for help against that which you describe." \[3\]

&#91;Pause. Silence for two seconds.\]

&#91;Section card: "Sold in Egypt."\]

A caravan stops at the well. Their water-drawer lowers his bucket — and shouts, "Good news! Here is a boy." \[3\] They hide him as merchandise and sell him cheaply, for a few dirhams. \[3\]

He ends up in Egypt, in the house of an important man, who tells his wife to treat him well — perhaps he'll benefit us. \[5\] And when Yusuf grows up, the Quran says Allah gave him judgment and knowledge. \[3\]

&#91;Section card: "The test."\]

Then comes a hard test. The wife of his master tries to seduce him. He refuses and says, "I seek the refuge of Allah." \[3\] When the truth is examined, his shirt is found torn from the back — proof he was pulling away, not advancing. \[3\] He's innocent. But he still ends up in prison, and he tells Allah he would rather have prison than what he is being called to. \[5\]

Think about that choice. Comfort with sin, or prison with his integrity. He chose prison.

&#91;Section card: "The prison and the king's dream."\]

In prison, two young men tell him their dreams, and he interprets them. \[3\] But before he does, he speaks to them about faith — he says he follows the religion of his fathers, Ibrahim, Ishaq and Yaqub. \[6\] Even in a cell, he's teaching.

Later, the king of Egypt has a disturbing dream: seven fat cows being eaten by seven lean ones. \[6\] Nobody can explain it. Then one of those former prisoners remembers Yusuf.

Yusuf's interpretation is practical: seven years of good harvest, then seven years of hardship. Store the grain in the ear so it lasts. \[5\] It's not just a prophecy — it's a food-security plan.

And the king asks for him. Yusuf asks to be put in charge of the storehouses of the land, saying he is a knowing guardian. \[5\] The boy from the well now manages Egypt's food.

&#91;Section card: "The brothers return."\]

When the hard years come, famine reaches Yaqub's family too. The brothers travel to Egypt to buy food — and stand in front of Yusuf. He recognises them. They don't recognise him. \[5\]

The Quran tells of how he brings his younger brother to stay with him, and of their father's grief, which grows so deep his eyes turn white with sorrow. \[5\] And still Yaqub says: I only complain of my suffering and my grief to Allah. \[5\] He never gives up hope.

&#91;Section card: "No blame upon you today."\]

Finally, the moment. The brothers stand before him again, humbled. He asks if they remember what they did to Yusuf. And they realise. "Are you indeed Yusuf?" And he says: "I am Yusuf, and this is my brother." \[5\]

They admit their wrong. And his reply is the line this whole story builds to: "No blame will there be upon you today. Allah will forgive you." \[5\]

&#91;Music swells gently, then fades.\]

No revenge. No speech about everything he suffered. Forgiveness, immediately.

He sends his shirt to his father, and when it's cast over Yaqub's face, his sight returns. \[5\] The family comes to Egypt. Yusuf raises his parents up to the throne, and they all bow down — and he says, this is the meaning of my dream from before. \[5\] The eleven stars, the sun and the moon.

&#91;Outro.\]

The surah ends by saying there is a lesson in these stories for people of understanding. \[5\] Patience from Yaqub. Integrity from Yusuf in the house of his master. Trust in Allah in the well and in the prison. And at the end, forgiveness — when he had every power to take revenge.

If this story stayed with you, share it with someone who needs it this week. And subscribe — next on The Golden Thread, we go to the journey that started the Islamic calendar: the Hijrah.

### OC-V1 — 1816, the Year Summer Never Came (Mon 30 Nov)

**Title:** 1816: The Year Summer Never Came

**Thumbnail:** snowy New England farm, calendar page "JUNE 1816." Text: "Snow in June?"

**Description:** In 1816, it snowed in June across New England. The cause was a volcano on the other side of the world — and that cold summer also gave us Frankenstein. Sources below.

**Tags:** year without a summer, 1816, Mount Tambora, Tambora eruption 1815, volcanic winter, Frankenstein origin, history mysteries, strange history

**Chapters:** Snow in June · Tambora erupts · How a volcano cools the planet · The summer of 1816 · What a cold summer created · Could it happen again?

**Music:** Audio Library → Cinematic, Mood: Dark; switch to Mood: Inspirational for the Frankenstein section.

**Voiceover**

&#91;Cold open: a hand brushing snow off green bean leaves. Wind.\]

Imagine waking up in June, walking out to your fields, and finding snow on your crops. Not frost. Snow. In 1816, across New England, that really happened — about half a foot of it, in a single day, in June. \[1\]

People at the time had no idea why. The cause was on the other side of the planet, and it had happened a year earlier.

This is the story of the year without a summer.

&#91;Title card. Map zooms from the US across the Pacific to Indonesia.\]

In 1815, Mount Tambora stood on the island of Sumbawa, in what's now Indonesia — then part of the Dutch East Indies. \[2\] It was a tall stratovolcano, around 14,000 feet, and it had been quiet for centuries. \[3\]\[2\]

Then, in April 1815, it woke up. The eruption reached its violent peak on the 10th of April. \[2\] It went on for about two weeks. \[1\]

To put it on a scale: volcanologists use the Volcanic Explosivity Index. Mount St. Helens and Vesuvius both score a 5. Krakatoa scores a 6. Tambora is a 7. \[4\] It's the most powerful eruption in recorded human history. \[2\]

&#91;Visual: animated bars VEI 5, 6, 7 — each step drawn ten times bigger.\]

Around the mountain, the human cost was enormous. Tens of thousands of people died from the eruption and the famine that followed; EarthDate, a programme from the University of Texas, puts the figure near 100,000. \[1\] We'll keep that with the respect it deserves, and follow how the effect travelled.

&#91;Music shifts. Visual: a globe with a thin grey veil spreading in the upper atmosphere.\]

Here's the part most people miss. It wasn't mainly the ash that changed the weather. Tambora pushed huge amounts of sulfur dioxide high into the atmosphere. Up there it turns into tiny droplets of sulfuric acid, and those droplets reflect sunlight back into space — for years. \[3\]

Less sunlight in, cooler planet. So the following year, 1816, the Northern Hemisphere got what scientists now call a volcanic winter — right through spring and summer. \[5\]

&#91;Section card: "The summer of 1816."\]

In May 1816, frost killed many of the crops already planted in the northeastern United States. \[5\] Then came that June snowfall. Farmers replanted, and lost crops again. Food got scarce and prices rose on markets from Ireland to China. \[6\]

And the strange thing? The world didn't connect the dots. Most people didn't link the bad weather to a volcano in Indonesia until decades later. \[6\] To them it was just a cursed year.

Meanwhile, something odd was happening up north. While much of the world cooled, the Arctic warmed so much that a lot of the sea ice around Greenland melted. \[3\] Climate doesn't move as one block. It shuffles.

&#91;Section card: "What a cold summer created."\]

Now for my favourite bit, because a miserable summer gave us some remarkable things.

In June 1816, a group of young writers were staying on Lake Geneva in Switzerland. The weather was so gloomy they were stuck indoors, and they held a contest — who could write the best ghost story. \[5\]

One of them was a nineteen-year-old named Mary Shelley. The story she started that summer became *Frankenstein*. \[1\] Another guest, John Polidori, wrote *The Vampyre*, one of the first vampire stories in English. \[5\]

&#91;Visual: candlelit room, rain on window, a quill.\]

Art changed too. Sunsets after a big eruption can be spectacular, because the particles scatter the light. Art historians believe those glowing skies influenced the British painter J. M. W. Turner and his famous hazy, luminous colours. \[3\]

There's even a theory about bicycles. In 1817, a German inventor named Karl Drais showed off his "running machine" — a two-wheeler you pushed along with your feet. Some historians link it to the horse-feed shortages after the failed harvests, though that connection is debated. \[4\] So file that under "maybe."

&#91;Section card: "Could it happen again?"\]

The good news first. The atmosphere cleaned itself up within about a decade, and life went back to normal. \[1\]

And Tambora? It's still active. Its last eruption was in 1967, a small one. \[4\] Scientists keep watching volcanoes like it, because an eruption that size would hit food supplies worldwide. It's possible. It's just not something experts expect in our lifetimes. \[3\]

&#91;Outro.\]

So the next time someone complains that summer was "a bit cold this year" — tell them about 1816. Snow in June. Crops gone twice. And a teenager in a rainy Swiss house, writing a monster story that's still in print two hundred years later.

If you enjoy the strange chapters of history that never made the textbook, subscribe. Next time: a flood of molasses that swept through the streets of Boston.

It's lunchtime on a Wednesday in January, in Boston. The North End is busy: the harbour, the warehouses, an elevated railway running past.

And then, without warning, a giant steel tank, fifty feet tall, splits open — and more than two million gallons of molasses come out all at once. \[1\]\[2\]

This really happened. It's called the Great Molasses Flood. And as strange as it sounds, it changed the way America builds things.

&#91;Title card.\]

&#91;Section card: "The tank on Commercial Street."\]

The date was the 15th of January, 1919, at about 12:30 in the afternoon. The place: the North End of Boston, at 529 Commercial Street. \[1\]

The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, which had been bought in 1917 by a bigger firm, the United States Industrial Alcohol Company. \[1\] Why so much molasses? Because molasses can be fermented into ethanol — used in alcoholic drinks, and also a key ingredient in munitions. \[1\] During the First World War, that was big business.

&#91;Visual: map of the North End, the tank's location marked beside the harbour and rail line.\]

The tank was built in 1915, about 50 feet tall and 90 feet across, able to hold 2.3 million US gallons — about 8.7 million litres. \[3\] It was put right by the harbour, where ships brought molasses in from Puerto Rico and further south, and close to the railway tracks. \[4\]

Three days before the disaster, on the 12th of January, a ship pumped 1.3 million gallons into it. \[5\] On the day it failed, it held roughly 2.3 million gallons, weighing around 26 million pounds. \[6\]

&#91;Section card: "The warning signs."\]

And there were warning signs. People living nearby said the tank leaked — molasses oozed down the seams. Locals even collected it. The company's response, according to accounts of the disaster, was to paint the tank brown, which made the leaks harder to see. \[7\]

&#91;Pause.\]

You don't need to be an engineer to see the problem with that solution.

&#91;Section card: "12:30 pm."\]

When the tank failed, the wave was enormous. Estimates put it somewhere between 15 and 40 feet high, moving at around 35 miles an hour. \[8\] History Today describes it as a wave about 15 feet high and 160 feet wide. \[6\]

&#91;Visual: archive photo of the twisted elevated railway girders.\]

It knocked buildings off their foundations. It bent the steel supports of the elevated railway. It wrecked a fire station. \[9\]

Twenty-one people died. About 150 were injured. \[2\]

&#91;Section card: "Why molasses is so dangerous."\]

Now here's the part that makes this disaster so unusual. Water flows away. Molasses doesn't — not easily. It's thick and heavy. In fact, per gallon, molasses weighs almost one and a half times as much as seawater. \[6\]

And it was January. As molasses cools, it gets thicker and stickier. So once the wave had spread out and slowed down in the cold air, people caught in it found it almost impossible to move. Rescuers struggled to reach them. The same thing that made it sweet made it deadly.

&#91;Section card: "Who was to blame?"\]

So whose fault was it? The company's lawyers had an answer ready: it wasn't us. They claimed the tank had been bombed by Italian anarchists. \[4\] This was 1919; fear of radical bombings was real, and it was a convenient story.

But the court didn't buy it. \[4\]

Families of the victims and people who had been injured filed 119 claims, and those were combined into what the Massachusetts Secretary of State calls the largest class action lawsuit in the state's history. \[5\]

&#91;Visual: newspaper headline clippings, 16 January 1919.\]

The case is remembered for something else too. It was one of the first to use expert witnesses — engineers and scientists explaining in court why the tank failed. \[2\]

&#91;Section card: "The rules it changed."\]

And this is why a sticky, almost comic-sounding disaster matters. NPR describes the case as the catalyst for new regulations governing architects and engineers. \[2\] Britannica notes that states across the country went on to adopt stricter construction codes. \[10\]

In other words: the next time someone signs off on a big tank, a bridge or a building, someone qualified has to check the calculations and stand behind them. That idea feels obvious now. In 1919, Boston learned it the hard way.

&#91;Section card: "The site today."\]

If you walk through the North End today, there's not much to see. There's a small plaque where the tank once stood. \[2\] And there's a baseball field — which, by coincidence, roughly echoes the round shape of the tank that stood there. \[11\]

&#91;Outro.\]

Twenty-one lives lost, in one of the strangest disasters in American history — and a shift in how engineering is regulated that still protects people today.

**Description:** A mechanical engineer explains the Panama Canal: a water staircase powered by gravity and rain — and why a drought can choke world trade. Sources below.

**Voiceover**

&#91;Cold open: a container ship sitting inside a lock, walls towering over it.\]

A ship as long as three football fields gets lifted 26 metres — that's roughly an eight-storey building — and there isn't a single pump doing the lifting. \[1\]\[2\]

I'm a mechanical engineer, and the first time I really understood how the Panama Canal works, I had to sit back for a minute. Because the whole thing runs on gravity. And rain.

Let me show you.

&#91;Title card. Map of Panama: Caribbean to the north, Pacific to the south.\]

First, the problem. Panama is narrow, but it isn't flat. A ridge of hills — the Continental Divide — runs right through it. A sea-level canal would have meant digging through all of it. The French tried a sea-level route in the 1880s, and it ended in failure. \[1\]

The American design that opened in 1914 went over the hills instead of through them. \[1\]

&#91;Animation: side profile — sea level, a "staircase" up to a lake, across, and down.\]

Here's the trick. Engineers dammed the Chagres River and created Gatun Lake, an artificial lake sitting 26 metres, about 85 feet, above sea level. \[1\] It covers roughly 425 square kilometres. \[3\] Ships don't cross the country at sea level. They climb up to the lake, sail across it, and climb back down.

The climbing is done by locks. On the Atlantic side, the Gatun Locks lift ships in three steps — 85 feet in total. On the Pacific side, it's split: the Miraflores Locks do two steps, and Pedro Miguel does one. \[2\]

A full crossing is about 80 kilometres and takes eight to ten hours. \[4\]

&#91;Section card: "How a lock lifts a ship."\]

Okay, the fun part. A lock is a concrete box with gates at each end. The original chambers are about 305 metres long and 33.5 metres wide. \[3\]\[1\]

A ship sails into the lower chamber. The gates close behind it. Now we need to raise the water.

Where does that water come from? From the lake above. Gatun Lake is higher than the chamber, so the operators open valves, and water flows downhill through huge tunnels — culverts — that run under the chamber floor, and rises up through openings in the floor. \[5\]

&#91;Animation: water flowing from the lake through culverts under the chamber, the ship rising.\]

No pumps. Gravity does it. The water in the chamber rises until it matches the next step, the gates open, and the ship moves forward. Repeat.

Going down is the same idea in reverse: you let water out of the chamber, toward the sea.

&#91;Hand-drawn sketch moment: a simple energy diagram — rain → lake → chamber → ship.\]

From an engineering point of view, this is elegant. The energy for lifting a fully loaded cargo ship comes from rain that fell on the hills and collected in a lake. The machinery mostly just opens and closes valves and gates.

&#91;Section card: "The catch."\]

But there's a cost. Every time a ship goes through, the water that lifted it flows out to the ocean. On average, one transit uses about 200 million litres of fresh water. \[6\] At around 40 transits a day, the canal can use something like 10 billion litres daily. \[7\]

&#91;Visual: counter ticking up in litres over a slowly dropping lake.\]

So the canal depends completely on rainfall. In 2023 and 2024, a severe drought dropped Gatun Lake to historic lows, and the canal had to cut daily transits and limit how deep ships could sit in the water. \[4\] Fewer ships, lighter loads, long queues on both oceans.

And then the opposite: in March 2026, the canal authority said Gatun and Alajuela reservoirs were at their highest March levels in its recorded history. \[7\] Same canal, totally different year. Rain decides.

&#91;Section card: "The upgrade."\]

In 2016, Panama opened a third set of locks for much bigger ships, called Neopanamax. Construction ran from 2007 to 2016, and commercial operation began on the 26th of June 2016. \[1\] The newer system can reuse some of the water used to move vessels — the original locks can't. \[8\]

When the canal opened in 1914 it handled about a thousand ships a year. By 2008 it was more than 14,700. \[1\]

&#91;Outro: a ship leaving the last lock into open sea.\]

So here's what I want you to remember: one of the most important shipping routes in the world is, at its heart, a water staircase powered by rain on a hillside. Brilliant design — and a reminder that every machine has a limit, and sometimes the limit is the weather.

**Title:** Why the World's Tallest Building Is Shaped Like a Y

**Thumbnail:** top-down floor plan of the Burj Khalifa glowing as a "Y", wind arrows bending around it. Text: "It confuses the wind."

**Description:** At 828 m, the Burj Khalifa's biggest enemy isn't gravity — it's wind. A mechanical engineer explains the buttressed core and how the tower "confuses the wind." Sources below.

**Voiceover**

&#91;Cold open: slow tilt up the Burj Khalifa from street level into cloud.\]

The Burj Khalifa is 828 metres tall. 163 floors. \[1\] And when people ask me how it stands up, they usually assume the hard part is holding up all that weight.

It isn't. Gravity is the easy part. The real enemy of a building this tall is the wind.

&#91;Title card.\]

&#91;Section card: "How wind shakes a tower."\]

Here's what wind actually does to a tall building. When air flows past any object — a pole, a chimney, a skyscraper — it doesn't just push. It peels off the sides in little swirls, called vortices. They break away first on one side, then the other, then the first side again.

&#91;Animation: top view of air flowing past a square tower, alternating swirls peeling off left and right.\]

Every time a vortex breaks away, it gives the building a small sideways tug. Left, right, left, right. The Burj's structural engineer, Bill Baker of the firm SOM, put it simply: when wind passes an object, it creates vortices that can cause the building to sway. \[2\]

And if those tugs arrive at the same rhythm as the building's natural sway — like pushing a child on a swing at exactly the right moment — the movement builds up. As an engineer, that's the scenario you design against.

&#91;Section card: "The Y-shaped plan."\]

So look at the Burj from above. It isn't a square or a circle. It's a Y — three wings coming out from the centre.

&#91;Visual: floor plan; the Y highlighted.\]

The engineers chose that shape on purpose, to reduce wind forces on the tower and keep the structure simple to build. \[3\] A Y-shaped floor plate also resists twisting much better than a square or rectangle would. \[4\]

&#91;Section card: "The buttressed core."\]

Inside, there's a system Baker and his colleagues developed called the buttressed core. In the centre is a strong hexagonal concrete core. Each of the three wings acts as a buttress, propping it up. \[5\]

Baker has a lovely way to explain it: picture someone holding an umbrella and leaning into the wind. Their front leg is the core. Their back leg is the buttress. \[6\] Each wing supports the others — so whichever direction the wind comes from, at least one wing is bracing the centre.

&#91;Hand-drawn sketch moment: a stick figure with an umbrella, legs labelled "core" and "buttress."\]

And here's what I love as an engineer: it's built from conventional materials and conventional construction methods. \[7\] No exotic alloys. The genius is in the geometry.

&#91;Section card: "Confusing the wind."\]

Now the clever part. Remember those alternating vortices? They only become dangerous when they organise — when they peel off in a steady rhythm along the whole height of the building.

The Burj steps back as it rises. The wings get shorter in a spiral pattern, so the shape of the building keeps changing as you go up. \[4\] At each height, the wind meets a slightly different cross-section, so the vortices can't all line up into one rhythm.

&#91;Animation: the tower rotating as setbacks spiral up; swirls at different heights shown out of sync.\]

The designers' phrase for this is "confuse the wind." \[4\]\[1\] The tower's orientation was even chosen to reduce the most problematic airflow on the site. \[4\]

They tested all of this in wind tunnels and reshaped the tower based on what they found. \[2\]

&#91;Section card: "Taller, not harder."\]

And here's a twist I didn't expect when I first read about it. The tower wasn't originally meant to be this tall. An early design was around 518 metres. \[8\] But as the engineers refined the shape for wind, they realised they could go taller while actually reducing wind forces and cost. \[8\]

The result is what Baker calls a very quiet building — he says it moves much less than many buildings half its height. \[8\] It beat the previous record holder, Taipei 101, by more than 60 percent. \[7\]

&#91;Section card: "Living in a Y."\]

The Y works for people, too. Three wings mean more windows and more views, which matters for apartments. \[6\] And with three wings and six faces, only about one-sixth of the building faces the sun at any given time, which helps keep it cooler. \[9\]

&#91;Outro: night shot of the tower lit up.\]

So next time you see a picture of the Burj Khalifa, don't look up. Look down at its footprint. That Y is the whole trick: a core, three buttresses, and a shape that keeps changing so the wind never finds its rhythm.

**Voiceover**

&#91;Cold open: timelapse of a dark sky, meteors streaking across.\]

In the middle of December, there's a night when you can step outside, look up, and — if your sky is dark enough — see a shooting star every minute or so. Sometimes more.

It's the Geminid meteor shower. And 2026 is a particularly good year to catch it. Let me tell you exactly when, where, and how.

&#91;Title card.\]

&#91;Section card: "When."\]

The Geminids peak on the night of the 13th into the 14th of December, 2026. \[1\]\[2\] But you don't need to hit one exact minute. The shower has a broad peak — rates of more than 100 meteors per hour can last 10 to 12 hours. \[3\] And it's active for days either side: roughly the 4th to the 17th of December. \[3\]

You'll see slightly different peak times quoted. EarthSky gives a predicted peak around 5:44 UTC on the 14th. \[1\] The International Meteor Organization's forecast, reported by Star Walk and Spain's National Geographic Institute, centres it later that day, around 14:00 UTC. \[3\]\[4\] My advice: don't stress about the exact hour. Go out on the night of the 13th, and if you can, the night of the 14th too.

&#91;On screen: "Peak night: 13–14 Dec 2026" with times for New York, London and Karachi.\]

&#91;Section card: "Why 2026 is a good year."\]

The biggest enemy of a meteor shower is the Moon. A bright Moon washes out the fainter meteors. This year, the Moon is only a thin waxing crescent on peak night. \[1\]\[2\] In the UK, the Royal Observatory says it sets around 9:20 in the evening — after that, the sky is properly dark. \[2\] And the next first-quarter Moon doesn't arrive until the 17th. \[1\]

So in 2026, the Moon is basically giving you the night off.

&#91;Section card: "Where to look."\]

The meteors seem to fly out from one point in the sky called the radiant, in the constellation Gemini, near its two bright stars, Castor and Pollux. \[5\]

&#91;Sky chart: Gemini with Castor and Pollux, arrows radiating outward.\]

But here's a tip a lot of people miss: you don't need to stare at Gemini. Meteors can appear anywhere in the sky; they just trace back to that point. Look at a wide, dark patch of sky, about halfway up.

The Geminids also have a nice bonus: they're the one major shower that shows good activity before midnight. \[2\] Many sources say the best time is around 2 a.m. local time, when the radiant is high. \[1\] But you'll see meteors from mid-evening onward.

They favour the Northern Hemisphere, but they're also visible from southern latitudes. \[6\]

&#91;Section card: "How many you'll really see."\]

Now, honesty time. You'll often read "120 meteors per hour." That's the zenithal hourly rate — what you'd see under a perfectly dark sky with the radiant straight overhead. \[7\] The American Meteor Society lists a theoretical maximum of around 150. \[8\]

In real life, you'll see fewer. Under a dark rural sky, maybe 75 to 150 an hour; from the suburbs, more like 25 to 50. \[5\] That's still a lot of shooting stars. City centre? You'll catch a few of the brightest ones. Get out of town if you possibly can.

&#91;Section card: "How to watch."\]

You don't need a telescope or binoculars — they'd actually make it worse, because they shrink your view. Just your eyes.

&#91;Visual: checklist animation.\]

Find somewhere dark, away from streetlights. Dress far warmer than you think — December nights are cold, and you'll be sitting still. A reclining chair or a blanket on the ground lets you look up without hurting your neck. Put your phone away, or turn on its red-light or night mode, because every glance at a bright screen resets your eyes' adjustment to the dark. Give yourself at least 20 minutes for your eyes to adapt, and then be patient. Meteors come in clusters, then quiet spells.

&#91;Section card: "An asteroid's dust."\]

One more thing that makes the Geminids special. Most meteor showers come from comets — icy objects leaving trails of dust. The Geminids' parent is an asteroid called 3200 Phaethon. \[3\]

The meteors are tiny bits of debris from Phaethon hitting our atmosphere at around 35 kilometres per second. \[4\] That's the light you see — a grain of space dust burning up high above you.

Why an asteroid behaves like a comet is a genuinely odd story, and we'll do a full video on Phaethon next week, before the peak.

&#91;Title card.\]

&#91;Section card: "One moment, worldwide."\]

First, the date. The December solstice in 2026 happens on Monday, the 21st of December, at 20:50 UTC. \[1\]\[2\] That's 3:50 in the afternoon in New York, 8:50 in the evening in London. \[3\]

&#91;Section card: "What a solstice is."\]

At that moment, the Sun reaches its southernmost point in our sky for the year. \[5\] It's directly overhead at about 23.5 degrees south — the Tropic of Capricorn. \[6\]

&#91;Animation: Earth orbiting the Sun, axis tilted, the overhead-Sun point sliding north and south through the year.\]

After that moment, the Sun starts heading north again. That's why so many cultures saw this as a turning point: the light is coming back.

&#91;Section card: "It's all about the tilt."\]

Why does it happen? Not because we're farther from the Sun. In fact, Earth is closest to the Sun in early January — in 2026, perihelion fell on the 3rd of January. \[7\] So winter in the north happens while we're nearly at our closest.

The real reason is tilt. Earth's axis is tipped over by about 23.4 degrees. In December, the Northern Hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun. Sunlight arrives at a low angle, spreads out over more ground, and the days are short. Six months later, the north tilts toward the Sun, and it flips.

&#91;Hand-drawn sketch moment: a torch beam on paper — straight-on makes a small bright circle; tilted makes a big dim oval.\]

&#91;Section card: "Shortest day here, longest there."\]

So the same moment means opposite things depending on where you live. In the north, it's the winter solstice and the shortest day. &#91;Section card: "The earliest-sunset puzzle."\]

Now, that puzzle from the start. If the 21st is the shortest day, why isn't it also the earliest sunset?

Because our clocks and the Sun don't keep perfectly the same time. Over the year, the moment the Sun is highest in the sky — solar noon — drifts earlier and later against the clock. EarthSky explains that this mismatch between clock time and Sun time is why the Northern Hemisphere's earliest sunset comes before the December solstice. \[9\]

The order is always the same: earliest sunset in early December, shortest day at the solstice, latest sunrise around early January. \[7\] The exact dates depend on your latitude. \[7\]

So if your evenings already feel like they're getting a tiny bit longer before Christmas — you're not imagining it.

&#91;Section card: "Why the date moves."\]

You might also see the solstice called "December 21st" as if it's fixed. It's not. Because our calendar year and Earth's orbit don't line up perfectly, the December solstice falls between the 20th and the 23rd. \[10\] The next one on the 20th of December won't happen until 2080 — and the next on the 23rd not until 2303. \[10\]

&#91;Section card: "Solstice through history."\]

People have marked this turning point for thousands of years. In Scandinavia, there was the pre-Christian Feast of Juul — Yule — at the time of the December solstice. \[8\] And under the old Julian calendar, the winter solstice fell on the 25th of December. \[8\]

&#91;Visual: old calendar pages, candlelight.\]

&#91;Outro: sunrise timelapse.\]

So on the 21st of December, take a second at sunset. The Sun has gone as far south as it will go this year — and from here, the days only get longer.

**Thumbnail:** chat bubble with a fake court stamp, a gavel beside it. Text: "$5,000 mistake."

**Description:** Two lawyers were fined after a chatbot invented court cases. Why AI "hallucinates," what OpenAI's own researchers say causes it, and three habits that protect you. Sources below.

**Voiceover**

&#91;Cold open: screen recording of someone typing a question; a confident answer appears instantly.\]

In 2023, two New York lawyers handed a federal judge a legal brief full of court cases. Real-sounding names, real-sounding quotes. There was just one problem. The cases didn't exist. A chatbot had made them up. \[1\]\[2\]

It cost them a $5,000 fine and a lot of embarrassment. \[1\] And it's the clearest example I know of something every AI user needs to understand: chatbots sometimes invent things, and they do it with total confidence.

Let's look at why that happens — in plain English.

&#91;Title card.\]

&#91;Section card: "The case."\]

The case was called *Mata v. Avianca*. A passenger, Roberto Mata, sued the airline Avianca, saying he was hurt by a serving cart on a 2019 flight from El Salvador to New York. \[3\]\[4\]

His lawyers, Steven Schwartz and Peter LoDuca, filed a brief citing six earlier cases. Schwartz had used ChatGPT to help with the research. He even asked the chatbot whether the cases were real — and it said yes. \[4\]

&#91;Visual: mock-up of a brief with six case names highlighted, labelled "reconstruction."\]

They weren't. When the airline's lawyers and the court couldn't find them, the judge, P. Kevin Castel, started asking questions. On the 22nd of June 2023 he fined the lawyers and their firm $5,000. \[2\] He wrote that they had "abandoned their responsibilities" — and he was especially unhappy that they kept standing by the fake cases after the court raised doubts. \[2\]\[1\] They were also ordered to send the judge's opinion to the real judges whose names the chatbot had attached to the fake rulings. \[5\]

&#91;Pause.\]

So how does a machine that seems so smart produce something completely fake?

&#91;Section card: "How a chatbot writes."\]

Here's the core idea. A chatbot like ChatGPT is a language model. During training, it read an enormous amount of text and learned to predict what word, or piece of a word, is likely to come next.

&#91;Animation: a sentence building word by word, each next word chosen from a short ranked list.\]

When you ask it something, it isn't looking up a fact in a database by default. It's generating the most plausible-sounding continuation, one piece at a time. Most of the time, plausible and true are the same thing. That's why it's useful.

But when it doesn't really "know" — say, a rare court case, or someone's birthday — plausible and true split apart. And it can produce something that looks exactly like a real answer.

&#91;Section card: "Why it guesses instead of saying 'I don't know'."\]

In September 2025, researchers at OpenAI published a paper called "Why Language Models Hallucinate." \[6\] Their argument is surprisingly simple. They compare it to a student taking a multiple-choice exam.

If you don't know an answer and you leave it blank, you get zero. If you guess, you might get lucky. So on a test that only counts right answers, guessing always looks better. \[7\]

&#91;Visual: exam sheet — blank answer = 0 points, guess = "maybe 1 point."\]

The researchers argue that the way AI models are trained and scored works the same way. Most tests reward accuracy alone, so models learn to guess rather than admit uncertainty. \[6\]\[7\] They even give a real example: when they asked a widely used chatbot for the PhD dissertation title of one of the paper's own authors, it confidently produced three different answers. None were correct. \[7\]

Their suggested fix is to change the scoreboards — penalise confident wrong answers more than honest "I don't know"s. \[7\] Companies say newer models hallucinate less, but nobody claims the problem is gone. \[7\]

&#91;Section card: "What this means for you."\]

So what should you actually do? Three habits.

One: treat a chatbot like a very fast, very confident assistant who sometimes bluffs. Great for drafts, ideas and explanations. Not a final source.

Two: anything that matters — a name, a date, a number, a quote, a law — check it somewhere independent. If the tool gives you links, open them and make sure they say what it claims.

Three: asking the chatbot "are you sure?" is not a check. Remember, in the Avianca case, that's exactly what the lawyer did. \[4\]

&#91;Outro.\]

AI tools are genuinely useful, and on this channel we'll keep testing them honestly — what works, what doesn't, and why. Next time: how a chatbot actually builds its answer, token by token. Subscribe if you want AI explained without the hype.

### PM-V2 — How a Chatbot Actually Writes Its Answer (Thu 3 Dec)

**Title:** How ChatGPT Actually Writes an Answer (Tokens, Explained Simply)

**Thumbnail:** a sentence split into coloured blocks, one block glowing as "next." Text: "It writes one piece at a time."

**Description:** Chatbots don't think in words — they work in tokens. What a token is, how a model picks the next one, and why it explains so many AI quirks. Sources below.

**Tags:** how ChatGPT works, what are tokens, AI tokens explained, large language models explained, next token prediction, AI for beginners, tokenizer, how AI writes

**Chapters:** It writes one piece at a time · What a token is · Rules of thumb · Red vs red · How the next token is chosen · Why tokens explain AI quirks · Outro

**Music:** Audio Library → Electronic, Mood: Bright; light and clean.

**Voiceover**

&#91;Cold open: screen recording of a chatbot answer appearing word by word.\]

Watch a chatbot answer you. It doesn't appear all at once. It streams, piece by piece, like someone typing really fast.

That's not just a visual effect. That's genuinely how it works. It's building the answer one small piece at a time. And those pieces aren't quite words. They're called tokens.

Once you understand tokens, a lot of strange AI behaviour suddenly makes sense. Let's go.

&#91;Title card.\]

&#91;Section card: "What a token is."\]

OpenAI describes tokens as the units its models use to process text. A token can be a single character, part of a word, a whole word, or punctuation. Even spaces affect how text gets divided up. \[1\]

&#91;Animation: the sentence "Chatbots are surprisingly predictable" splitting into coloured chunks; "surprisingly" splits into two.\]

Common short words are usually one token. Longer or rarer words get split into pieces. And the split doesn't always follow where words start and end — tokens can include a trailing space, or just part of a word. \[2\]

The method most of these models use is called subword tokenization. OpenAI's GPT models use a version called Byte-Pair Encoding. \[3\] You don't need to remember the name — just the idea: text gets chopped into reusable chunks.

&#91;Section card: "Rules of thumb."\]

How big is a token? For English, OpenAI's rough rules are: one token is about four characters, or about three-quarters of a word. So 100 tokens is roughly 75 words. \[1\]

&#91;On screen: "1 token ≈ 4 characters ≈ ¾ of a word"; "100 tokens ≈ 75 words."\]

Other languages split differently. OpenAI's own example: the Spanish phrase "Cómo estás" — ten characters — comes out as five tokens. \[2\] That's one reason the same message can "cost" a different number of tokens in different languages.

&#91;Section card: "Red vs red."\]

Here's a detail I find fascinating. To the model, the same word can be different tokens depending on how it's written.

In OpenAI's tokenizer, " Red" — with a space before it and a capital R — is token number 2296. " red" with a lowercase r is token 2266. \[4\] Different numbers. To the machine, those are two separate things it has learned are closely related.

&#91;Visual: two blocks, " Red" = 2296 and " red" = 2266, with a thin line between them.\]

So when you type, the model never sees letters the way you do. It sees a list of numbers.

&#91;Section card: "How the next token is chosen."\]

Now for the actual writing. OpenAI lays out three steps: your text is split into tokens, the model processes those tokens, and then the response is generated as a sequence of tokens, which is converted back into text. \[5\]

&#91;Animation: input text → numbers → a box labelled "model" → a ranked list of candidate next tokens → one gets picked and added → repeat.\]

That last step is the heart of it. At each step, the model predicts what should come next, and then selects one. \[3\] Then it adds that token to everything so far, and predicts again. And again. Hundreds of times for a long answer.

It never plans the whole paragraph in advance the way you might. It's always answering one small question: given everything so far, what's the most fitting next piece?

&#91;Section card: "Why tokens explain AI quirks."\]

This explains a lot.

It's why chatbots have length limits — they're measured in tokens, not words, and each model has a maximum it can handle at once. \[6\]

It's why AI pricing for developers is per token. \[7\]

And it's a big part of why chatbots can be oddly bad at things that seem easy, like counting the letters in a word. The model doesn't see letters — it sees chunks. We'll do a whole video on that one.

It also connects to Monday's video about AI making things up. If the model is always picking the most fitting next piece, then when it doesn't truly know a fact, it can still produce something that fits perfectly — and is completely wrong.

&#91;Outro: the streaming answer from the cold open, now with token blocks overlaid.\]

So the next time you watch an answer stream onto your screen, you'll know what's happening: a very fast machine, choosing one small piece at a time.
